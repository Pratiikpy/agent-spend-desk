## 1. How the process works today

The sequence below is a composite of the sourced fragments quoted in it. The composite itself, and in particular the claim that the steps are owned by different teams with no single point that reconciles them at the moment of payment, is the product premise. It is NOT VERIFIED by this research; it needs practitioner interviews (section 8).

| # | Step | Typical owner | Typical controls | Failure modes (sourced where possible) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Request and business case | Requester, budget owner | Budget approval, sourcing policy | Pressure and urgency are the attack surface for impersonation (AFP, below). |
| 2 | Due diligence and selection | Procurement, compliance or TPRM team | Registry and ownership check, sanctions check, financial condition, licences, security questionnaire | Self-reported questionnaire answers; registries that are themselves self-declared (section 4). |
| 3 | Vendor master creation | Master-data team or AP | Four-eyes on creation, duplicate-vendor check | Shell or duplicate vendors (ACFE glossary: "employee creates a shell company and bills employer for services not actually rendered"). |
| 4 | Vendor master change (bank details) | AP, with callback by treasury | Out-of-band callback, change approval | Vendor impersonation by email. AFP 2025: "Vendor imposter fraud, cited by 45% of respondents". |
| 5 | PO, goods receipt, invoice | Buyer, receiving, AP | Three-way match | Matches quantities and documents, not the identity of the payee (see below). |
| 6 | Payment approval and release | AP, treasury, approvers | Dual approval above thresholds, segregation of duties, bank-side screening | Split invoices under thresholds; rubber-stamp approval; screening misses altered names (FI test). |
| 7 | Ongoing monitoring and exit | TPRM, internal audit | Rescreening, periodic review | Stale due diligence; list changes after onboarding. |

Sourced fragments behind the table:

- Lifecycle. The US interagency guidance names five stages: "1. Planning 2. Due Diligence and Third-Party Selection 3. Contract Negotiation 4. Ongoing Monitoring 5. Termination". Source: Federal Register, 9 June 2023, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-06-09/html/2023-12340.htm. It applies to banking organisations (Federal Reserve cover letter SR 23-4, https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2304.htm). A corporate buyer is not bound by it, but it is the most detailed public statement of what a due-diligence file should contain.
- Due-diligence content. Same document, legal and regulatory compliance: "evaluating the third party's ownership structure (including identifying any beneficial ownership, whether public or private, foreign, or domestic ownership)" and "determining whether the third party itself or any owners are subject to sanctions by the Office of Foreign Assets Control". This is the closest regulator text to what Plumbline reconciles.
- Three-way match. Odoo documentation: "The 3-way matching feature ensures vendor bills are only paid once some (or all) of the products included in the PO have been received." (https://www.odoo.com/documentation/18.0/applications/inventory_and_mrp/purchase/manage_deals/control_bills.html). Critical reading: the matched objects are the PO, the receipt and the bill. Nothing in that description checks that the bank account on the bill belongs to the legal entity that was diligenced. That is an observation about one ERP's documentation, not a survey of ERPs.
- Billing schemes and rerouting. ACFE 2026 glossary: billing scheme is "submitting invoices for fictitious goods or services, inflated invoices, or invoices for personal purchases"; check and payment tampering includes "employee re-routes an outgoing electronic payment to a vendor to be deposited into their own bank account" (https://www.acfe.com/-/media/files/acfe/pdfs/rttn/2026/2026-report-to-the-nations.pdf).
- BEC definition. FBI IC3 2025 report, Appendix B: "BEC is a scam targeting businesses or individuals working with suppliers and/or businesses regularly performing wire transfer payments." Note this definition covers more than vendor bank-detail change; the IC3 loss figure cannot be read as the vendor-change loss.
- Lookalike domains. AFP 2026 (via Nacha summary, https://www.nacha.org/news/business-email-compromise-attempts-rose-sharply-2025-report-finds): "More than half of respondents" reported receiving emails from nearly identical domains with minor character variations.
- Controls that work. ACFE 2026: "all 18 controls were associated with reduced median losses and faster detection. Among these, four controls: management review, proactive data monitoring and analysis, a code of conduct, and surprise audits were associated with the largest reductions in both measures." Also: "the presence of an internal control REDUCED FRAUD LOSSES BY MORE THAN ONE-THIRD (37% average reduction in losses for controls present vs. not present)". Association, not causation: organisations with controls differ in other ways.
- Collusion defeats four-eyes. ACFE 2026: "When fraudsters colluded with external co-conspirators, including customers or vendors, median losses were higher than when they colluded with other employees." Segregation of duties is necessary and not sufficient.
- Recovery after the fact is weak. AFP 2025: "22% recovered 75% or more (down from 41% in 2023)". Preventing release is worth more than clawing back.

What the process does not give a buyer, as far as the sources read show: a single record, at release time, that states which facts about the payee are verified, which conflict, which are absent, and which rule decided.

Sanctions screening quality: one regulator measurement and one folk number.

- Measurement. Finansinspektionen (Sweden), 6 November 2024, in-depth analysis of 19 banks, https://fi.se/contentassets/6090223c4c3e4de98aadedc6721afe7c/in-depht-analysis-sanction-screening-2024.pdf. Each bank screened "a test list of 5,000 names from the UN's and the EU's sanctions lists". Customer screening: spelled as listed, "on average 86.3 per cent; the median value was 99.3 per cent"; names manipulated, "on average 63.9 per cent, and the median was 73.4 per cent"; "the highest effectiveness for manipulated names in the customer screening was 96.1 per cent while the lowest effectiveness was 5.0 per cent"; "none of the banks achieved 100 per cent". Natural persons 82.9 per cent against legal persons 98.4 per cent on correctly spelled names. False alerts: "4.9 per cent of the cases in customer screening" on average, one bank at 51.5 per cent because of its test settings. Limits: test environment, Swedish banks, UN and EU lists only, the "false alert" definition is the regulator's (share of hits against non-sanctioned test names) and is not the industry's alert-to-true-match ratio.
- Folk number. The claim that name screening has 85 to 95 per cent false positives circulates in vendor and trade writing. I found no regulator or academic primary source for it in this session, and the FI measurement above defines the quantity differently and finds 4.9 per cent. Do not publish the 85 to 95 figure. If the grant needs a false-positive statement, use the FI numbers with their definition, or measure our own.

Not found: a primary-source estimate of the annual cost of releasing payment to the wrong counterparty as such. The master spec already holds this as NOT VERIFIED; that stands. The defensible impact statement is a conjunction of sourced facts (IC3 BEC losses, AFP incidence, ACFE billing medians, FI recall on altered names), each with its caveat, not a single headline number.

### 3.3 The gap Plumbline fills, stated so it can be falsified

Claimed gap: no tool among those read combines all of (a) reconciliation of internal master data against external registries with an explicit `unresolved` state, (b) claims whose cited quotes are mechanically checked against stored source text before any model judges them, (c) deterministic policy plus precedent memory with a fail-closed approval gate, and (d) an offline-verifiable, replayable decision dossier. Existing tools do pieces of (a) (Quantexa), workflow and monitoring (OneTrust), payee verification (Trustpair), and claim-sourcing assertions (Grep AI).

Falsification test, to run before the grant text says "first" or "only": obtain documentation or a demo for Grep AI, Quantexa and one TPRM suite and test whether any (i) rejects a fabricated quotation that has a real URL, (ii) separates "source silent" from "source contradicts", (iii) exports a bundle verifiable offline. If any does, the claim narrows to what remains. Until then the wording is "we did not find".

## 5. Regulatory and standards mapping

"Meets by construction" means the design makes it true without extra work. "Designed" means the mechanism is in the spec but is not built or measured, so it is NOT VERIFIED. Nothing here is a compliance claim. Citations are to the text read; unread items say UNVERIFIED.

### 5.1 Is a counterparty-release system in scope of the EU AI Act as high-risk?

Reading of the text (not legal advice; LAWYER for a binding view):

- Annex III lists eight areas. Read at https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/. Counterparty or vendor release appears in none of them. The nearest entries: 4(b) "AI systems intended to be used to make decisions affecting terms of work-related relationships, the promotion or termination of work-related contractual relationships ... or to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons in such relationships"; 5(b) "AI systems intended to be used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score, with the exception of AI systems used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud".
- Conclusion on the text: a system whose intended purpose is releasing payments to business counterparties is not an Annex III use case as listed, so Article 6(2) does not make it high-risk. Edge cases that need a lawyer: (i) vendors who are sole traders or individual contractors (4(b) work-related relationships; 5(b) if financial-standing scoring of a natural person creeps in); (ii) screening and scoring of natural-person beneficial owners and officers, which is profiling; the last subparagraph of Article 6(3) (fragments read: "shall always be considered to be high-risk where the", then "profiling"; the full sentence was not captured, so read it before relying on it) makes Annex III systems that profile natural persons high-risk regardless of the derogation, which only bites if the system is in Annex III first.
- The intended purpose is a legal fact set by the provider's documentation (Article 3 definition read on the Article 12 page). Writing the intended purpose precisely, and excluding natural-person scoring, is a design and documentation decision.
- Timing. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (Digital Omnibus on AI) was "published in the Official Journal of the European Union on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026" (https://ertico.com/digital-omnibus-on-ai-enters-into-force, a secondary source; the Official Journal text was not read). It sets application for stand-alone Annex III systems at 2 December 2027. The artificialintelligenceact.eu Article 12 page shows the same date "according to Article 113(c)". Any older "August 2026" statement is outdated.
- Consequence for the grant: we can truthfully write that the system is designed to meet the high-risk requirements voluntarily, because that is what funders and buyers will look for, and that its legal classification is a stated assumption pending counsel. We must not write "AI Act compliant".

### 5.3 Honest summary of coverage

Met by construction once built and tested: record-keeping (Art. 12(1)), traceable outputs, replay, separation of proposal from action, default-deny approvals, untrusted-text handling, measurement and reporting of accuracy. None of these is verified today; the spec itself says nothing is built.

Not met and not currently designed: governance and accountability (NIST Govern, ISO 42001), data-protection analysis (GDPR Art. 22 and 35), retention and deletion, vendor-facing explanation and tipping-off, Annex IV-style documentation, instructions for use with declared accuracy, bias examination of name matching, measurement of reviewer over-reliance. These are the bulk of what a responsible-AI reviewer will probe.

Out of scope and to be stated as out of scope: human-rights and environmental supply-chain due diligence, verification of beneficial ownership, bank-account ownership verification, contract negotiation and termination stages of TPRM.

## 6. Ten domain-realism requirements for the design and the gold set

Each says what to include and the measurement it enables. Sources are the figures above; the scenario designs are mine.

1. Name matching at regulator difficulty. Build the sanctions gold set the way FI built its test: take real list entries (with licensing handled), run correctly spelled and manipulated variants ("removing a letter, replacing a letter with another, or modifying the listed person's date of birth", per the FI report), and add what the regulator did not state it tested but practice requires: transliteration variants, diacritics, word order, honorifics, legal-form suffixes and abbreviations for companies, and aliases. Report recall at a fixed alert budget, split natural versus legal persons. Baselines: Watchman, yente, plain edit distance. Target to beat is not "100 per cent"; FI found none of 19 banks reached it.
2. Negative names that look like positives. Common-name collisions, same surname plus different birth year, companies sharing a trade word. A matcher that only maximises recall is useless; the decision cost of a false hold is real. Include the "Series A versus Series B" class already in the spec.
3. Vendor bank-detail-change fraud, in full. Scenarios: email from a near-identical domain asking to change IBAN (AFP: more than half receive such domains); change request that includes its own "call this number to confirm" (callback to the number in the request versus the number on file); change filed shortly before a large payment; change to an account in a different country from the registered address; change by the same user who later approves the payment; legitimate changes (bank merger, new treasury centre) that look the same. The correct system behaviour is a hold with the specific unmet verification, never a silent pass.
4. Split-invoice threshold evasion. Invoices individually under the dual-approval threshold, same vendor and similar amounts inside a rolling window, or split across vendor records that share a bank account. Aggregation window and threshold must be policy parameters in the rule engine, with a counterfactual showing the aggregate would have required approval.
5. Duplicate and shell vendors. Two vendor ids with the same account, same address with different names, vendor address equal to an employee address (a standard shell-company indicator; the ACFE glossary example is an employee who "creates a shell company and bills employer for services not actually rendered"). Requires the internal silo to carry employee-adjacent attributes, synthetic and labelled.
6. Three-way match with legitimate noise. Partial receipts, price variance inside tolerance, goods received after invoice date, credit notes. The gold set needs many legitimate mismatches that should release and fraudulent matches that look perfect (a fake invoice that matches a real PO). Odoo's description shows match is on quantities and documents, so identity facts are what Plumbline adds.
7. Segregation of duties and collusion. Same person creates vendor, changes bank details, and approves; approver is requester's manager; two approvers who always approve together. ACFE finds collusion raises median loss, so a four-eyes check can fail. Plumbline needs actor identities in the case and a rule that separates roles, plus a pattern view over past approvals.
8. Ownership structure realism. Multi-layer holding chains, nominee and trust holders, "nature of control" without a shareholding, undisclosed or unavailable owners, and aggregation across owners (a sanctioned owner below any single-owner threshold but above it in aggregate). The relevant aggregation rule for US sanctions is not verified here; read OFAC's guidance before encoding a threshold. Ownership thresholds differ by regime and must be per-policy parameters, not constants.
9. Time and staleness. A vendor clear at onboarding who is designated later; register status changes; list versions between onboarding and release. Release-time re-screening against a dated list version is the default; the record states list version, retrieval time and age. Phoenix companies (dissolved and re-registered under a similar name) belong here.
10. Realistic prevalence and asymmetric cost. Real cases are overwhelmingly legitimate. A gold set at 50 per cent fraud flatters every metric. Evaluate at several prevalences (for example 0.1, 1 and 10 per cent), report precision, recall, and an expected-cost curve with explicit costs for a false hold (late payment, supplier friction) and a false release (loss, partly recoverable: AFP 2025 reports only 22 per cent recovered 75 per cent or more). Also include pressure cases (an executive demanding urgent release) to test whether the approval gate holds. The cost parameters are assumptions and must be published as such.

## 7. Three contributions a grant reviewer would value

All three are hypotheses until the named test passes. Novelty relative to the literature is NOT VERIFIED here beyond the prior-art claim in `04` section 8.

A. An open benchmark for evidence-grounded counterparty release with realistic difficulty. The planned open gold set (master spec 16.5) gains three properties from this research: perturbation protocols modelled on a regulator's own published test (FI 2024), realistic prevalence and cost-weighted scoring (requirement 10), and labels that separate "source silent" from "source contradicts" at claim level. Deliverables: generator, labels, annotation protocol, datasheet, scoring code, baselines (Watchman, yente, Splink, a single long-context prompt), confidence intervals. Why it is valuable: reviewers can reproduce it, and it measures the part that regulators already measure and vendors do not publish.

B. Measuring human oversight, not asserting it. Art. 14(4)(b) names automation bias; most systems implement an approval button and stop. Plumbline can seed known defects into dossiers (a swapped quotation, a stale sanctions list version, an unresolved ownership conflict marked resolved) under a protocol and measure whether approvers catch them, with and without the claim ledger and counterfactual panel, and use the catch rate in the Wilson-bound autonomy promotion. Result: an oversight-efficacy metric and an ablation of which explanation features help. Constraint: needs human participants or at least informed volunteers; ethics review and consent are required; sample sizes in a grant are small, so report intervals and say so.

C. Audience-tiered, proof-preserving explanations. Explainability (Art. 86, internal audit, regulators) conflicts with confidentiality on sanctions and fraud hits (tipping off). Produce one decision dossier with committed hashes and let three views be derived: reviewer (full), counterparty (reason code only, no list or source detail), regulator or auditor (full, or redacted with commitments that let the offline verifier confirm the redacted dossier is consistent with the full one). Deliverable: a redaction scheme, a verifier, and a test that no tier leaks fields it should not. Legal acceptability of any tier is LAWYER; the technical contribution stands without it.

Also worth stating as a reviewer-facing argument, not a contribution: the fail-visible design (nine fetch states, `unresolved`, gap ledger) answers the main criticism of LLM-based due-diligence tools, which is silent confidence. It earns credit only with the evidence-corruption results.

What funders ask for. The one funder policy read this session is NLnet (https://nlnet.nl/propose/ and https://nlnet.nl/foundation/policies/generativeAI/). Quotes: "Software, hardware and content must be released under open licences and apply (preferably open) standards"; grants "range from €5,000 to €50,000 for initial applications" (as summarised from the page); GenAI policy in force 8 December 2025, version 1.1 of 26 January 2026: applicants "may use GenAI tools in preparing applications, but any such use must be disclosed", a "prompt provenance log must be maintained" listing "the model used, dates and times of prompts, the prompts themselves, the unedited output", and "outcomes purely generated by AI are not allowed to be submitted as work eligible for payment (as part) of the grant". The policy "concerns GenAI specifically and does not regulate other forms of automation" and the page's own "next deadline" line read November 3, 2026.

Implication that is easy to miss: Plumbline is being built with AI coding assistants. For an NLnet-type funder, that is not disqualifying but it is disclosable, requires a prompt log going forward, and means the grant must pay for human-directed work (design, evaluation, review), not for generated code. The hackathon repository history cannot be reconstructed into a prompt log after the fact. Decide now whether a grant path is wanted and, if so, start the log. Other funders (Horizon Europe-type, responsible-AI foundations) were not read; which requirements apply to them is UNVERIFIED. My synthesis of what any applied-AI reviewer will want, flagged as synthesis and not as a verified funder list: reproducibility from a clean checkout, a written evaluation protocol, a safety or risk case, data governance and licence clarity for every dataset, open outputs under a recognised licence, and honest limitations.
